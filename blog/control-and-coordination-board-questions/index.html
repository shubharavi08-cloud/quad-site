Control and Coordination: 7 Board Question Types, Solved (CBSE Class 10 Science Chapter 6)

By Team QUAD14 Nov 20267 min read

▶ Control and Coordination Class 10: important board questions, solved · open on YouTube

Chapter 6 of CBSE Class 10 Science, Control and Coordination, looks easy because it has few numbers and no equations. Students still lose marks here, for three reasons: mixing up the direction in which a nerve impulse travels, naming the wrong part of the brain for a function, and writing "hormone" without naming the gland it comes from.

Below are the seven question types that keep coming back. Read each one, pause and try it yourself, then check the model answer. The video above walks through the same kind of questions.

Type 1: The neuron and the path of a nerve impulse

Draw the structure of a neuron and explain how information travels through it.

  • Information is picked up at the end of the dendrites of a nerve cell (neuron).
  • It sets off a chemical reaction that creates an electrical impulse.
  • The impulse travels from the dendrite to the cell body and then along the axon to its end.
  • At the end of the axon, the electrical impulse releases chemicals. These cross the tiny gap called the synapse and start a similar impulse in the dendrite of the next neuron.

Order to remember: dendrite → cell body → axon → nerve ending → synapse → next neuron.

Labels to show: dendrite, cell body, nucleus, axon, nerve ending.

Try it: what is a synapse? The gap between the nerve ending of one neuron and the dendrite of the next, where the impulse is carried across by chemicals.

Type 2: Reflex action and the reflex arc

What is a reflex action? Draw and label a reflex arc.

  • A reflex action is a sudden, automatic response to a stimulus that happens without thinking, for example pulling your hand away from a hot plate.
  • The pathway it uses is the reflex arc: receptor (in the skin) → sensory neuron → relay neuron in the spinal cord → motor neuron → effector (muscle).
  • Reflex arcs are formed in the spinal cord, so the response is quick. The information still travels on to the brain, which becomes aware of what happened.

Common mistake: writing that the brain controls a reflex. The quick response is decided in the spinal cord.

Try it: why have reflex arcs evolved? Thinking takes time. In danger, a fast automatic response protects the body before the brain has processed the signal.

Type 3: Parts of the brain and their functions

This is often asked as a match-the-column or "which part controls..." question.

| Part | Region | Main functions | |---|---|---| | Cerebrum | Forebrain | Thinking, memory, learning; receives sensory information (sight, hearing, smell); controls voluntary muscle movement | | Hypothalamus | Forebrain | Controls hunger, thirst and body temperature; controls the pituitary gland | | Cerebellum | Hindbrain | Keeps posture and balance; makes voluntary movements precise (walking in a straight line, riding a bicycle, picking up a pencil) | | Medulla | Hindbrain | Controls involuntary actions: blood pressure, salivation, vomiting | | Pons | Hindbrain | Involuntary actions, including breathing | | Midbrain | Midbrain | Involuntary actions, including some reflex movements of the head, neck and eyes |

How the brain and spinal cord are protected: the brain sits inside the skull (cranium) in a fluid-filled balloon that absorbs shocks; the spinal cord is protected by the vertebral column (backbone).

Try it: which part of the brain helps you keep your balance on a moving bus? The cerebellum.

Type 4: How plants respond without nerves

How does the sensitive plant (touch-me-not) respond to touch? How is this different from the movement of a shoot towards light?

  • Touch-me-not (Mimosa pudica): the leaves fold and droop at once when touched. Cells pass the information on by electrical and chemical means, and the plant cells change shape by changing the amount of water in them. There is no growth involved.
  • Growth movements (tropisms): slow, directional movements caused by growth.

| Tropism | Stimulus | Shoot | Root | |---|---|---|---| | Phototropism | Light | Bends towards light | Bends away from light | | Geotropism | Gravity | Grows upward, away from gravity | Grows downward, towards gravity | | Hydrotropism | Water | — | Grows towards water | | Chemotropism | Chemicals | Pollen tube grows towards the ovule | — |

Try it: a tendril of a pea plant coils around a stick. Why? The part of the tendril away from the support grows faster than the part touching it, so it curls around the stick.

Type 5: Plant hormones

Name the plant hormone that (a) helps cells grow longer, (b) promotes cell division, (c) inhibits growth.

| Hormone | What it does | |---|---| | Auxin | Made at the shoot tip; makes cells grow longer. When light comes from one side, auxin moves to the shaded side, so that side grows faster and the shoot bends towards light | | Gibberellins | Help the stem grow | | Cytokinins | Promote cell division; found in large amounts where cells divide fast, such as fruits and seeds | | Abscisic acid | Inhibits growth; causes wilting of leaves |

Marking tip: in the phototropism answer, the mark is for saying auxin moves to the shaded side and that side grows faster. Without both, the answer is incomplete.

Type 6: Endocrine glands and their hormones

Name the gland and the hormone in each case, and say what goes wrong when it is not made in the right amount.

| Gland | Hormone | Function / what goes wrong | |---|---|---| | Pituitary | Growth hormone | Controls growth; too little in childhood causes dwarfism | | Thyroid | Thyroxin | Controls the use of carbohydrates, proteins and fats; needs iodine, so iodised salt prevents goitre | | Pancreas | Insulin | Controls blood sugar level; too little causes diabetes | | Adrenal | Adrenaline | Prepares the body for an emergency: faster heartbeat, more oxygen to the muscles, less blood to the digestive system and skin | | Testes | Testosterone | Changes at puberty in males | | Ovaries | Oestrogen | Changes at puberty in females |

Try it: why does the heart beat faster when you are frightened? The adrenal glands release adrenaline into the blood. It acts on the heart and muscles to prepare the body to respond.

Type 7: Feedback mechanism

How is the timing and amount of hormone release controlled? Explain with an example.

  • Hormones are released in the right amount at the right time by a feedback mechanism.
  • Example: when the blood sugar level rises, cells of the pancreas detect it and release more insulin. As the sugar level falls, insulin secretion is reduced.

Compare nervous and hormonal control: nerve impulses are fast but reach only the cells connected by nerves, and a nerve cell cannot keep sending impulses continuously. Hormones travel in the blood, are slower, but can reach all cells of the body and act for longer.

Five-minute revision checklist

  • Impulse: dendrite → cell body → axon → synapse (chemicals cross the gap).
  • Reflex arc: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord (relay) → motor neuron → muscle.
  • Cerebrum thinks, cerebellum balances, medulla and pons run involuntary actions.
  • Skull protects the brain; backbone protects the spinal cord.
  • Touch-me-not: water and cell shape, no growth. Tropisms: growth.
  • Auxin bends shoots to light; cytokinins divide; abscisic acid stops growth.
  • Growth hormone, thyroxin (iodine), insulin (sugar), adrenaline (emergency). Feedback keeps the amount right.

FAQ

Is Control and Coordination important for the boards? Yes. Expect a diagram (neuron or reflex arc), at least one plant-movement question and a gland-hormone table or case question.

Which diagrams should I practise? The neuron, the reflex arc and a labelled human brain. Practise each until you can draw and label it in three minutes.
