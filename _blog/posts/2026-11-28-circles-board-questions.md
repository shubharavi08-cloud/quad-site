Chapter 10 of CBSE Class 10 Maths, *Circles*, is one of the shortest chapters in the book and one of the most reliable scorers. In the rationalised NCERT book it is only about **tangents**: what a tangent is, and two theorems about it. Every board question in the chapter is built from those two theorems, so once you can spot where they apply, the marks come quickly.

Board papers test the chapter in a steady pattern: **1-mark MCQs and an assertion-reason** on tangent angles and lengths, a **2- or 3-mark question** on a circle inside a triangle or a chord with tangents, a **proof** (often worth 3 to 5 marks), and sometimes a **case study**. The video above works through eight must-do questions of these kinds from recent CBSE papers and the 2026-27 Sample Paper. Below, each one has a model answer, the trap students fall into, and a quick question to try yourself.

## The two theorems you must know

| Theorem | What it says |
|---|---|
| Theorem 10.1 | The tangent at any point of a circle is **perpendicular to the radius** through the point of contact. |
| Theorem 10.2 | The **lengths of the two tangents** drawn from an external point to a circle are equal. |

Two quick facts follow from these. A circle has **exactly one tangent at each point** on it, and **two tangents from a point outside** it (none from a point inside). And in the quadrilateral formed by the centre, the two points of contact and the outside point, the **angle between the tangents and the angle at the centre add up to 180°**, because the other two angles are 90° each.

## Type 1: Tangents at right angles, find OP (1 mark)

> PQ and PR are tangents to a circle with centre O and radius 4 cm, and ∠QPR = 90°. Find OP.

- Join OQ and OR. Both are radii, 4 cm each, and each is perpendicular to its tangent (Theorem 10.1).
- So OQPR has four right angles, and PQ = PR (Theorem 10.2). With PQ = OQ = 4 cm, it is a **square**.
- OP is its diagonal: √(4² + 4²) = √32.
- **Answer: OP = 4√2 cm, option (b).**

**Trap:** adding 4 + 4 and picking 8 cm. OP is the diagonal of the square, not two sides joined together. **Try it:** the tangents from P meet at 90° and OP = 10√2 cm. Find the radius. *10 cm.*

## Type 2: Area of the kite PQOR (1 mark)

> PQ and PR are tangents from an external point P to a circle with centre O and radius 6 cm. If OP = 10 cm, find the area of quadrilateral PQOR.

- ∠OQP = 90°, so in right ΔOQP, PQ = √(10² − 6²) = √64 = 8 cm.
- Area of ΔOQP = ½ × 6 × 8 = 24 cm².
- ΔORP is congruent to it (OR = OQ, PR = PQ, OP common).
- **Answer: area of PQOR = 2 × 24 = 48 cm², option (a).**

**Trap:** writing 6 × 8 = 48 with no reason. The answer is the same, but the marking scheme wants the two triangles. **Try it:** radius 5 cm, OP = 13 cm. Find the area of PQOR. *60 cm².*

## Type 3: The angle at the centre and the angle between the tangents (1 mark)

> TP and TQ are tangents from an external point T to a circle with centre O. If ∠POQ = 120°, find ∠PTQ.

- ∠OPT = ∠OQT = 90° (radius perpendicular to tangent).
- The angles of quadrilateral OPTQ add up to 360°: 120° + 90° + 90° + ∠PTQ = 360°.
- **Answer: ∠PTQ = 60°, option (a).**

**Trap:** halving 120° to get 60° "by luck". That is the inscribed-angle rule, which does not apply here. Use the 180° rule and you will get the right answer every time, even when the numbers change. **Try it:** the tangents from T meet at 70°. Find ∠POQ. *110°.*

## Type 4: Assertion-reason on the radius (1 mark)

> **Assertion (A):** The radius is the shortest distance of a tangent from the centre of the circle.
> **Reason (R):** The radius of a circle is perpendicular to the tangent at the point of contact.

- R is Theorem 10.1, so R is true.
- Every other point of the tangent lies outside the circle, so its distance from O is more than the radius. A is true.
- The shortest distance from a point to a line is the perpendicular, and R says the radius *is* that perpendicular. So R explains A.
- **Answer: option (a), both true and R is the correct explanation of A.**

**Trap:** choosing (b) out of habit. Ask "does R tell me *why* A is true?" Here it does. **Try it:** how many tangents can a circle have at one point on it? *Exactly one.*

## Type 5: A circle inside a right triangle (2 marks)

> A circle is inscribed in a triangle ABC, right-angled at B, with AB = 8 cm and BC = 15 cm. Find the radius of the circle.

- AC = √(8² + 15²) = √289 = 17 cm.
- The radii to AB and BC, with ∠B = 90°, make a square of side r at the corner B.
- So the tangent lengths are r from B, (8 − r) from A and (15 − r) from C.
- Tangents from A and from C are equal, so AC = (8 − r) + (15 − r): 17 = 23 − 2r.
- **Answer: r = 3 cm.**

**Trap:** using (a + b + c)/2 = 20. That is the semi-perimeter, not the radius. For a right triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c, the inradius is r = (a + b − c)/2. **Try it:** legs 6 cm and 8 cm. Find the inradius. *2 cm.*

## Type 6: A chord and the tangents at its ends (3 marks)

> AB is a chord of length 24 cm of a circle of radius 15 cm. The tangents at A and B meet at P. Find PA.

- OP is perpendicular to AB and meets it at its mid-point M, so AM = 12 cm.
- In right ΔOMA: OM = √(15² − 12²) = √81 = 9 cm.
- ∠OAP = 90° (radius perpendicular to tangent) and AM ⊥ OP, so ΔOMA ~ ΔAMP (AA), which gives AM² = OM × MP.
- 144 = 9 × MP, so MP = 16 cm.
- PA = √(AM² + MP²) = √(144 + 256) = √400.
- **Answer: PA = 20 cm.**

**Trap:** assuming OP = OM + 15. P lies outside the circle, so MP has to be worked out, not guessed. **Try it:** a chord of 8 cm in a circle of radius 5 cm; the tangents at its ends meet at P. Find PA. *20/3 cm.*

## Type 7: The rhombus proof (3 to 5 marks, CBSE SQP 2026-27)

> Prove that a parallelogram circumscribing a circle is a rhombus.

- **Given:** parallelogram ABCD whose sides AB, BC, CD and DA touch a circle at P, Q, R and S. **To prove:** ABCD is a rhombus.
- Tangents from the same point are equal: AP = AS, BP = BQ, CR = CQ, DR = DS.
- Adding: (AP + BP) + (CR + DR) = (AS + DS) + (BQ + CQ), that is, **AB + CD = AD + BC**.
- In a parallelogram AB = CD and AD = BC, so 2AB = 2AD, and AB = AD.
- Adjacent sides are equal, so all four sides are equal. **ABCD is a rhombus.** Hence proved.

**Trap:** writing "AP = BP". Two tangents are equal only when they start from the *same* point. Name the four pairs correctly and the proof writes itself. **Try it:** a quadrilateral ABCD is drawn around a circle with AB = 6 cm, BC = 7 cm and CD = 4 cm. Find AD. *AB + CD = AD + BC, so AD = 3 cm.*

## Type 8: The case study (4 marks)

> A circular meditation garden has centre O and radius 9 m. Arjun stands at P, 18 m from O, and draws tangents PA and PB to the boundary. (i) Find ∠OAP. (ii) Find PA. (iii) Find ∠APB and ∠AOB.

- **(i)** Radius perpendicular to tangent, so **∠OAP = 90°**.
- **(ii)** PA = √(18² − 9²) = √243 = **9√3 m**.
- **(iii)** In right ΔOAP, sin ∠APO = 9/18 = ½, so ∠APO = 30° and **∠APB = 60°**. Then **∠AOB = 180° − 60° = 120°**.

**Trap:** stopping at ∠APO = 30° in part (iii). OP cuts the angle at P into two equal halves, so double it. **Try it:** radius 5 m and OP = 10 m. Find the angle between the tangents. *60°.*

## One-minute revision

- **Radius ⊥ tangent** at the point of contact. Draw that radius first in every figure.
- **Tangents from one outside point are equal**, and OP bisects the angle between them.
- Angle between the tangents + angle at the centre = **180°**.
- A quadrilateral drawn around a circle has **AB + CD = AD + BC**.
- In a right triangle, **inradius = (a + b − c)/2**.
- Learn both theorem proofs. Theorem 10.2 (equal tangents) is a favourite 3-mark proof.

## FAQ

**Which proofs from Circles are asked in the board exam?**
Mostly the two theorems themselves (the radius is perpendicular to the tangent, and the tangents from an external point are equal) and riders built on them, such as the rhombus proof above and "the angle between the tangents is supplementary to the angle at the centre".

**Is Circles a long chapter?**
No. In the rationalised NCERT book it has two exercises (10.1 and 10.2). That makes it one of the best chapters to revise in the last weeks before the board exam.
