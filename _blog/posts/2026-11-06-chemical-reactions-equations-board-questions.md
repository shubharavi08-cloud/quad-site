Chapter 1 of CBSE Class 10 Science, *Chemical Reactions and Equations*, is one of the most scoring chapters in the paper. Most marks here are lost not because students misunderstand the chemistry, but because of small things: **missing state symbols, an unbalanced equation, or an observation left out**.

This guide works through three question types that keep coming up in board papers, with model answers and the marking points to watch. As always: read the question, **pause and try it yourself**, then check.

## Question 1: Heating lead nitrate (3 marks)

> **(a)** Write a balanced chemical equation for the reaction that occurs when lead nitrate powder is heated in a dry boiling tube.
> **(b)** State two observations for this reaction.
> **(c)** Is the reaction endothermic or exothermic? Give a reason.

### (a) Balanced equation

<p class="eq">2Pb(NO<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>(s) <span class="ar">—heat→</span> 2PbO(s) + 4NO<sub>2</sub>(g) + O<sub>2</sub>(g)</p>

**Marking tip:** the coefficients 2, 2 and 4 must be clearly written. If the question asks for physical states, leaving out (s) and (g) can cost you part of the mark.

Check the balance yourself: on each side there are 2 Pb, 4 N and 12 O atoms.

### (b) Two observations

1. **Brown fumes** of nitrogen dioxide (NO<sub>2</sub>) are given off.
2. A **yellow residue** of lead monoxide (PbO) is left in the tube.

### (c) Type of reaction

It is **endothermic**: heat has to be supplied continuously to break the lead nitrate down. This is a **thermal decomposition** reaction.

## Question 2: Sodium sulphate and barium chloride (2 marks)

> What happens when an aqueous solution of sodium sulphate reacts with an aqueous solution of barium chloride? Write the balanced chemical equation and name the type of reaction.

- **Observation:** a **white precipitate** of barium sulphate (BaSO<sub>4</sub>) forms as soon as the solutions are mixed.
- **Equation:**

<p class="eq">Na<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>(aq) + BaCl<sub>2</sub>(aq) → BaSO<sub>4</sub>(s)↓ + 2NaCl(aq)</p>

- **Type:** a **double displacement** reaction. Because an insoluble solid forms, it is also called a **precipitation** reaction.

**Marking tip:** many students write "displacement". It is *double* displacement because the two compounds swap ions (Ba<sup>2+</sup> and Na<sup>+</sup> change partners).

## Question 3: The oxidising-agent trap

Students often mix up *the substance oxidised* with *the oxidising agent*:

- **Substance oxidised:** the reactant that **gains oxygen** (or loses hydrogen).
- **Oxidising agent:** the reactant that **gives the oxygen**. It is itself reduced.

Take this reaction:

<p class="eq">CuO + H<sub>2</sub> <span class="ar">—heat→</span> Cu + H<sub>2</sub>O</p>

- H<sub>2</sub> gains oxygen, so **H<sub>2</sub> is oxidised** to H<sub>2</sub>O. H<sub>2</sub> is the **reducing agent**.
- CuO loses oxygen, so **CuO is reduced** to Cu. CuO is the **oxidising agent**.

A quick way to remember it: the agent does the job to the *other* substance, and the opposite happens to the agent itself.

### Oxidation in everyday life

The same chapter asks about two everyday effects of oxidation:

- **Corrosion:** a metal is slowly attacked by air and moisture, like the rusting of iron.
- **Rancidity:** fats and oils in food get oxidised and start to smell and taste bad. Adding antioxidants, keeping food in airtight containers, and filling chip packets with nitrogen all slow it down.
