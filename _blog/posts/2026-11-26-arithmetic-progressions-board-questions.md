Chapter 5 of CBSE Class 10 Maths, *Arithmetic Progressions*, runs on just two formulas. That makes it one of the easiest chapters to score full marks in, as long as you avoid a handful of small, repeated mistakes. Board questions come in three kinds: a **1-mark MCQ on the nth term or the common difference**, a **2- or 3-mark question on the sum**, and a **word problem** where you have to spot the AP yourself.

## The two formulas you must know

For an AP with first term **a** and common difference **d = (any term) − (the term before it)**:

| What you want | Formula |
|---|---|
| nth term | aₙ = a + (n − 1)d |
| Sum of the first n terms | Sₙ = n/2 [2a + (n − 1)d] |
| Sum when the last term l is known | Sₙ = n/2 (a + l) |

## Type 1: Find the nth term (1 mark)

> Find the 20th term of the AP 3, 8, 13, 18, …

- a = 3, d = 8 − 3 = 5.
- a₂₀ = 3 + 19 × 5 = 3 + 95.
- **Answer: a₂₀ = 98.**

**Trap:** writing 20 × 5 instead of 19 × 5. The first term already counts as one step, so you add d only (n − 1) times. **Try it:** the 10th term of 2, 7, 12, …? *2 + 9 × 5 = 47.*

## Type 2: Which term is it? Is it a term at all? (1 to 2 marks)

> Which term of the AP 3, 8, 13, … is 78? Is 301 a term of the AP 5, 11, 17, 23, …?

- 3 + (n − 1) × 5 = 78, so (n − 1) × 5 = 75, n − 1 = 15 and **n = 16: 78 is the 16th term.**
- For 301: 5 + (n − 1) × 6 = 301, so (n − 1) × 6 = 296 and n − 1 = 49⅓.
- **Answer: n is not a whole number, so 301 is not a term of that AP.**

**Trap:** rounding 49⅓ to 49 and saying "the 50th term". A term number must be a positive whole number. **Try it:** is −150 a term of 11, 8, 5, 2, …? *11 − 3(n − 1) = −150 gives n − 1 = 53⅔: no.*

## Type 3: The nth term from the end (2 marks)

> Find the 20th term from the last term of the AP 3, 8, 13, …, 253.

- Read the AP backwards: it starts at 253 and the common difference becomes −5.
- 20th term from the end = 253 + 19 × (−5) = 253 − 95.
- **Answer: 158.**

**Trap:** counting from the front by mistake. Shortcut: nth term from the end = l − (n − 1)d. **Try it:** the 11th term from the last of 10, 7, 4, …, −62? *Here d = −3, so −62 − 10 × (−3) = −32.*

## Type 4: Three terms in AP, find k (2 marks)

> For what value of k are 2k + 1, 13 and 5k − 3 three consecutive terms of an AP?

- If a, b, c are in AP, then **2b = a + c**.
- 2 × 13 = (2k + 1) + (5k − 3), so 26 = 7k − 2.
- **Answer: k = 4.** Check: the terms are 9, 13, 17, with d = 4 each time.

**Trap:** setting the first term equal to the third. The rule is about the *middle* term: it is the average of its neighbours. **Try it:** find p if 2p, p + 10 and 3p + 2 are in AP. *2(p + 10) = 2p + 3p + 2 gives p = 6: the terms are 12, 16, 20.*

## Type 5: Sum of the first n terms (2 marks)

> Find the sum of the first 20 terms of 2, 7, 12, …

- a = 2, d = 5, n = 20.
- S₂₀ = 20/2 [2 × 2 + 19 × 5] = 10 × [4 + 95] = 10 × 99.
- **Answer: S₂₀ = 990.**

**Trap:** using 2a + nd. Inside the bracket it is always (n − 1)d, exactly as in the nth-term formula. **Try it:** sum of the first 15 terms of 1, 4, 7, …? *15/2 × [2 + 42] = 330.*

## Type 6: How many terms give this sum? (3 marks)

> How many terms of the AP 24, 21, 18, … must be taken so that their sum is 78?

- a = 24, d = −3. Sₙ = n/2 [48 + (n − 1)(−3)] = n/2 [51 − 3n] = 78.
- n(51 − 3n) = 156, so 3n² − 51n + 156 = 0, and dividing by 3, n² − 17n + 52 = 0.
- (n − 4)(n − 13) = 0, so n = 4 or n = 13.
- **Answer: 4 terms or 13 terms, and both are correct.** The 5th to 13th terms (12, 9, 6, 3, 0, −3, −6, −9, −12) add up to 0, so the sum comes back to 78.

**Trap:** throwing away n = 13. Both values are positive whole numbers, so both count, and the explanation earns marks. This is a favourite NCERT question in board papers.

## Type 7: Sₙ is given, find the nth term (2 to 3 marks)

> The sum of the first n terms of an AP is Sₙ = 4n − n². Find the first term, the common difference and the nth term.

- S₁ = 4 − 1 = 3, so a = 3.
- S₂ = 8 − 4 = 4, so a₂ = S₂ − S₁ = 1 and d = 1 − 3 = −2.
- aₙ = a + (n − 1)d = 3 − 2(n − 1).
- **Answer: a = 3, d = −2, aₙ = 5 − 2n.**

**Trap:** thinking S₂ is the second term. S₂ is the sum of the first *two* terms. Use **aₙ = Sₙ − Sₙ₋₁**. **Try it:** if Sₙ = 3n² + 5n, find the 25th term. *S₂₅ − S₂₄ = 2000 − 1848 = 152.*

## Type 8: The word problem (3 to 5 marks)

> A sum of ₹700 is to be used to give seven cash prizes to students for their overall academic performance. Each prize is ₹20 less than the prize before it. Find the value of each prize.

- The prizes form an AP with n = 7 and d = −20. Let the first prize be a.
- S₇ = 7/2 [2a + 6 × (−20)] = 700, so 2a − 120 = 200 and a = 160.
- **Answer: ₹160, ₹140, ₹120, ₹100, ₹80, ₹60 and ₹40.** Check: they add up to ₹700.

**Try it:** find the sum of all three-digit numbers divisible by 7. *The AP is 105, 112, …, 994 with d = 7, so n = (994 − 105)/7 + 1 = 128 and S = 128/2 × (105 + 994) = 70,336.*

## One-minute revision

- First find **a** and **d**, and write them down before you use any formula.
- nth term: a + (n − 1)d. Sum: n/2 [2a + (n − 1)d], or n/2 (a + l) when the last term is known.
- A term number must be a positive whole number. If you get a fraction, the number is not a term.
- Three terms in AP: twice the middle one equals the sum of the other two.
- aₙ = Sₙ − Sₙ₋₁ whenever the question gives you a formula for Sₙ.

## FAQ

**How many marks does Arithmetic Progressions carry?**
It is part of the Algebra unit of CBSE Class 10 Maths. Most papers have at least one MCQ or Assertion–Reason question on the nth term and a 2- or 3-mark question on the sum, and the chapter is a favourite for case-study questions.

**Do I need to learn the n/2 (a + l) formula separately?**
Yes. It saves a lot of time whenever the last term is given, for example in "sum of all two-digit multiples of 3" questions.
