Most Class 10 students solve previous-year questions in the last few weeks before the exam, in one long sitting, and then check only whether the final answer matches. That is the least effective way to use them. Used well, previous-year questions are the best map you have of how a board paper actually asks things. Here is a method that works for any subject.

## 1. Learn the chapter first, then test it

Previous-year questions are a test, not a textbook. If you open them before you understand the chapter, you will only copy solutions without knowing why they work. Finish the chapter, do the textbook examples, and only then move to past questions on that chapter.

## 2. Sort questions by type, not by year

Take one chapter and group its past questions: one-mark facts, short reasoning, long answers, diagram or numerical questions, and case-based questions. You will quickly see that a chapter keeps asking the same few kinds of things in slightly different clothes. Knowing the pattern is half the preparation.

## 3. Pause and try before you look

This is the rule we follow in every QUAD video: **pause and try first**. Spend a real attempt on each question, even if you are unsure. Struggling for two or three minutes teaches your brain far more than reading the answer straight away. Only then check the solution.

## 4. Mark your mistakes in three colours

After checking, mark each wrong answer as one of three kinds:

- **Concept gap**: you did not understand the idea. Fix it by re-learning the topic.
- **Method slip**: you knew the idea but chose the wrong steps. Fix it with more practice problems.
- **Careless error**: a sign, a unit, a copied number. Fix it with a checking habit.

Each kind needs a different fix, so do not lump them together.

## 5. Keep a one-page error list

For every chapter, write down your own three or four recurring mistakes on a single page. Read that page the night before the exam instead of rereading the whole book. It is short, personal, and aimed exactly at where you lose marks.

## 6. Practise writing answers, not just solving

Marks in a board exam depend on how you present the answer: the steps shown, the correct units, the labelled diagram, the reason given in science. Write at least some past questions out fully, as you would in the exam, and compare them with the marking-style answers your teacher or textbook shows.

## 7. Time yourself in the final weeks

Once the chapters are done, attempt a full paper in the actual exam time, without notes. Afterwards, note which section took too long. Time management is a skill, and the only way to build it is to practise under time.

## A simple weekly plan

Early in the week, finish one or two chapters. Midweek, solve the past questions for those chapters by type. At the weekend, review your error list and redo only the questions you got wrong. Small, regular work beats one marathon.

## A final thought

Previous-year questions do not guarantee that the same question will repeat. What they give you is familiarity with how questions are framed and how marks are earned. Combine that with understanding, and you walk into the exam calm instead of surprised.
